**Date of analysis:** 2026-06-13

## (a) Raw Content Summary

The preliminaries section is a terse, two-subsection block that establishes the formal scaffolding for everything that follows.

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

A combinatorial optimization (CO) problem with n discrete decision variables is formalized as:

- **Solution representation:** tuples `(a_1, ..., a_n)` where each `a_i` is a decision variable.
- **Solution space `S`:** the set of all possible tuples (feasible and infeasible).
- **Objective `f`:** maps `S` to real values (or `-inf` for infeasible solutions). The goal is to maximize `f`.
- **Constructive approach:** decisions are made sequentially: choose `a_1`, then `a_2` given `a_1`, and so on.
- **Policy network `pi_theta`:** computes conditional distributions `pi_theta(a_{d+1} | bar_a_{1:d})` for partial solutions.
- **Autoregressive construction:** partial solutions are built from the empty tuple. The joint probability factorizes as:
  - `pi_theta(bar_a_{1:d}) = prod_{i=1}^d pi_theta(a_i | bar_a_{1:i-1})`
- **Constant-length assumption:** the paper assumes all problem instances have the same length `n` for notational simplicity, noting that variable-length problems can be handled with a termination token or null action.

**Key design point:** the factorization means every decision is conditioned on the entire prior sequence (no Markov simplification).

### 3.2 Self-Improved Training Cycle

The self-improved learning (SIL) loop is the engine that drives the method without external expert demonstrations:

1. **Initialization:** parameters `theta` are randomly initialized. The "best-so-far" parameters `theta'` are set equal to `theta`.
2. **Per epoch — three phases:**

   **(Phase 1 — Data generation):** Random problem instances are generated. For each instance, the current best policy `pi_{theta'}` decodes *multiple* solutions using some decoding method (not specified here; this is the plug-in point for TaSaR). The best among these solutions, per the objective `f`, is kept. The collection of best-solution-per-instance forms the epoch's training set.

   **(Phase 2 — Policy update):** The policy is trained via cross-entropy (maximum-likelihood) loss on next-token prediction:
   ```
   L_theta = -(1/B) sum_{j=1}^B log pi_theta(a_{d_j+1}^j | bar_a_{1:d_j}^j)
   ```
   Batches use `B` solutions with *uniformly sampled partial solutions* — not full trajectories. This is important: the loss is over all positions in each solution, not just the first decision.

   **(Phase 3 — Evaluation / leader update):** Greedy (deterministic) decoding is run on a fixed validation set. If `pi_theta` outperforms `pi_{theta'}`, then `theta'` is updated to `theta`.

3. **Scale note:** thousands of instances per epoch are desirable.
4. **Critical remark:** "The decoding method strongly determines SIL efficiency."

## (b) Key Ideas Unpacked

### Idea 1: The factorization assumption is the foundation

The assumption that a CO solution can be represented as a fixed-length ordered tuple `(a_1, ..., a_n)` and built left-to-right is nontrivial. It imposes:

- A canonical **ordering of decisions** must exist or be imposed.
- Each decision is **conditioned on all prior decisions** (full autoregression).
- The **action space at each step** may vary: some choices prune or enable future options.

For classic routing problems (TSP, CVRP), this ordering is natural (visit cities in sequence). But for problems like VNE where the solution has multiple interdependent components (node mappings AND path routings), the ordering is an explicit design choice.

### Idea 2: The policy network is a conditional generative model of actions

`pi_theta(a_{d+1} | bar_a_{1:d})` is not a value estimator (critic) or a Q-function. It is a **purely constructive policy** that assigns probabilities to each legal action given the state so far. This means:

- It does not need to model the full solution quality at once.
- It only needs to discriminate among *currently available* alternatives.
- It is trained by maximum likelihood on whatever the best found solution was — it learns to imitate the *search result*, not an expert.

This is the key difference from actor-critic or value-based RL: the policy is trained with a **behavioral cloning** objective where the "teacher" is the search process itself.

### Idea 3: Self-improvement operates through a "leader" mechanism

`theta'` (the "leader") is retained across epochs and only updated when the student `theta` beats it on a validation set. This:

- Provides **training signal stability** — the student always learns from solutions generated by the *best-known* policy, not its current unsteady self.
- Creates a **non-increasing leader gap**: `theta'` is monotonically improving (or at least not getting worse) in terms of greedy validation performance.
- Is reminiscent of **self-play** in AlphaZero but without an explicit opponent; the "opponent" is the problem instance itself.

### Idea 4: The loss function is behavioral cloning on search-augmented demonstrations

The cross-entropy loss `L_theta` treats each partial solution as a training point. The key insight is:

- The targets come from **beam search / TaSaR** (a stronger procedure than the policy alone).
- Training on uniformly sampled partial positions means the policy learns to navigate **arbitrary depths of the search tree** — not just early decisions.
- This is strictly more informative than training only on the first decision or only on the full trajectories.

### Idea 5: The decoding method is the bottleneck

The remark "the decoding method strongly determines SIL efficiency" is the paper's thesis statement in miniature. The loop is:

```
weak policy → poor decoding → poor training data → weak policy (vicious cycle)
```

or

```
okay policy → good decoding → good training data → better policy (virtuous cycle)
```

The decoding method is the **amplifier** that converts a moderately good policy into high-quality training data. TaSaR is designed to be a better amplifier.

## (c) Technical Specifics

### Equation: Joint probability of a partial solution
```
pi_theta(bar_a_{1:d}) = prod_{i=1}^d pi_theta(a_i | bar_a_{1:i-1})
```

This is the autoregressive factorization. It means:
- The model must be **causal** (no future information leaks into predictions).
- Gradients flow through the entire sequence during training.
- Inference cost is O(n) for greedy decoding (one forward pass per decision).

### Equation: Cross-entropy loss
```
L_theta = -(1/B) sum_{j=1}^B log pi_theta(a_{d_j+1}^j | bar_a_{1:d_j}^j)
```

Where:
- `B` = batch size in terms of (partial solution, next action) pairs.
- `(bar_a_{1:d_j}^j, a_{d_j+1}^j)` = a single training pair from solution `j`.
- The `d_j` index is **uniformly sampled** across positions in the solution.
- This is equivalent to maximum likelihood estimation: we maximize the probability of the actions that the search process selected.

### Algorithm: Self-Improved Training Cycle

```
1. Randomly initialize theta; theta' ← theta
2. For each epoch:
   a. Generate M random instances
   b. For each instance i:
      - Decode K solutions using pi_{theta'} and some decoding method
      - Keep the best solution according to f
   c. Collect all best solutions → training set D
   d. Sample B (partial_solution, next_action) pairs from D
   e. Update theta by minimizing L_theta on these B pairs
   f. Run greedy decoding of pi_theta on validation set V
   g. If pi_theta outperforms pi_{theta'} on V: theta' ← theta
```

### Hyperparameter scales mentioned
- **"Thousands of instances per epoch"** are desirable. This is a scale note: the method works best when the training set is large.
- **Batch size B** is mentioned but no specific number is given (architecture dependent).
- **K** (number of decoded solutions per instance) is not specified here (it's the beam_width in TaSaR terms).

### Design choice: constant length n
The paper assumes constant `n` for simplicity but notes variable-length problems are handled with a termination token or null action. This is a modeling choice, not a limitation.

## (d) VNE Implications

### D1. Problem formulation mismatch — VNE is NOT a fixed-length ordered tuple

**Paper assumption:** a CO solution is `(a_1, ..., a_n)` with fixed `n`.

**VNE reality:** The solution has two intertwined components:
- **Node placements:** which virtual node maps to which substrate node.
- **Path routings:** which substrate path carries each virtual link.

The current VNE implementation reconciles this by using the **augmented formulation** from `PROBLEM_FORMULATION.md`: each action embeds one virtual link as a complete substrate path, and node placements arise implicitly as path endpoints. The sequence length equals the total number of virtual links across all requests, which is **variable** (depends on how many requests and how many processing nodes per request).

**Critical implication for VNE:**
- The paper's constant-length assumption is a convenience, not a requirement. Variable-length sequences are handled with a termination signal.
- But the VNE trajectory already has a natural termination condition (all requests fully embedded), which maps naturally to "no more actions."
- The autoregressive factorization `pi_theta(a_{d+1} | bar_a_{1:d})` is directly applicable: the state at step `d` includes all previous placements and residual resources.

### D2. Action space per step — MUCH larger than TSP

For TSP at step `d`, the policy chooses among `n-d` remaining unvisited cities. The action space is **size-variable but bounded by n**.

For VNE at step `d`, the policy chooses among **all feasible substrate paths** for the current virtual link, conditioned on residual resources. The number of simple paths in a graph with 20-40 communication nodes is **exponential in the worst case** (though in practice limited by connectivity and residual bandwidth).

**Impact on `pi_theta`:**
- The policy must score a **large and variable** candidate set each step.
- The current VNE implementation uses candidate-count bucketing (up to 8000 candidates) as a practical bottleneck.
- This is fundamentally harder than TSP/CVRP where the action set is elements already in the model's input.

### D3. The factorization assumption interacts with VNE solution equivalence

For TSP, the ordering `(city_1, city_2, ..., city_n)` has a clear meaning. For VNE, the ordering of virtual links can produce **equivalent solutions** under different permutations. The paper's factorization:

```
pi_theta(bar_a_{1:d}) = prod_{i=1}^d pi_theta(a_i | bar_a_{1:i-1})
```

does not address the question of **what order** the decisions should be made in. The current VNE chain formulation uses a fixed ordering (request by request, link by link within each request), but for general virtual graphs the ordering is an open design problem.

### D4. The self-improvement loop applies directly to VNE

The three-phase cycle maps to VNE without modification:

1. **Data generation:** The `GumbeldoreDataset` / Ray-based parallel data generation already generates random VNE instances and decodes multiple solutions using TaSaR. The best per instance is kept.

2. **Policy update:** The cross-entropy loss applies directly. The VNE replay data in `dataset.py` stores `(state, action)` pairs from solved solutions. The uniformly sampled partial solutions correspond to every `(request_idx, processing_link_idx)` decision in the trajectory.

3. **Validation:** The current validation pipeline (`vne_main.py`) already runs greedy (beam-1) decoding on validation instances and compares performance.

**Already implemented:** The current VNE codebase has all three phases wired up.

### D5. The leader mechanism (theta') is crucial for VNE training stability

The discovery from the HANDOFF.md (gradient clipping fixed the epoch-6 divergence) shows that VNE training is **inherently unstable**. The leader mechanism `theta'` in the self-improved cycle provides an additional smoothing effect: training data in epoch `e` is generated by `theta'` (the best version so far), not the current volatile `theta`. This means a bad parameter update in one epoch does not corrupt the next epoch's training data.

**However:** the current VNE supervised training mode does NOT use the leader mechanism (it trains on a fixed pre-solved dataset). Only the Gumbeldore mode (not yet fully run for VNE) would benefit from this self-stabilizing property.

### D6. The "uniformly sampled partial solutions" trick matters for VNE

VNE solutions are long: `sum_over_requests (num_processing_nodes - 1)` decisions. If training only on the first few decisions, the policy would never learn to handle the resource-exhausted late-game states.

**Insight:** Uniform sampling means the VNE training batch will include as many late-stage decisions as early ones. This is critical because:
- Early decisions are easy (abundant resources, many feasible paths).
- Late decisions are hard (scarce resources, few feasible paths, some paths lead to dead ends).
- Training on both phases ensures the policy learns the full trajectory distribution.

### D7. The "thousands of instances" requirement is an open challenge for VNE

The 50k dataset generation was already a significant engineering effort (SLURM array with shard merging). Each instance requires solving a MILP for labels. For Gumbeldore mode (search-generated data without ILP labels), the bottleneck shifts from solving MILPs to running TaSaR search, which is also expensive.

**Practical constraint:** VNE cannot easily scale to "thousands of instances per epoch" if each instance requires minutes of computation. The current 1k/50k datasets are static. For online generation (Gumbeldore mode), the Ray-based generation will need to be efficient.

## (e) Critical Insights for Our Work

### Insight 1: The paper's preliminaries provide the "what" but not the "how well"

The preliminaries define the training loop but give **no quantitative guidance** on:
- How many solutions per instance (beam width)?
- How many training pairs per epoch?
- What learning rate or schedule?
- How many epochs until saturation?

This means every decision about hyperparameters for VNE must be empirically determined. The paper's main contribution (TaSaR) is about the decoding method; the rest of the training infrastructure is inherited from Gumbeldore (the original codebase).

### Insight 2: The leader mechanism is the unsung hero of SIL stability

Many papers on self-improved learning gloss over the leader mechanism, but it is **essential**:
- Without it, the policy chases its own tail (generating training data from a degrading policy).
- With it, there's a monotonic improvement guarantee in terms of the leader's validation performance.
- **For VNE specifically:** given the observed training instability (epoch-6 divergence even with supervised data), the leader mechanism may be the difference between convergence and collapse in Gumbeldore mode.

### Insight 3: The factorization + cross-entropy combination means the policy is trained to be a "good imitator" of the search process

This is not RL (no reward-to-go, no value function, no advantage estimation). It is **behavioral cloning with a dynamically improving teacher**. The practical consequence:

- The policy will only be as good as the search process that generates its training data.
- If the search process has systematic biases (e.g., always prefers short paths for VNE, which may exhaust compute capacity), the policy will learn those biases.
- The validation leader update only tests *greedy* performance, not search performance. So a policy that imitates well but generalizes poorly could still update the leader and propagate flaws.

### Insight 4: The decoding method is the only differentiating factor between epochs

In the SIL loop, the only thing that changes between epoch `t` and epoch `t+1` search results is:
- `theta'` may have been updated (if validation improved).
- The random instances are fresh (no dataset reuse unless specifically done).

This means the **decoding method is the sole mechanism for creating improvement pressure**. If the decoding method (TaSaR) does not find solutions better than the current policy's greedy decode, the leader never updates and the loop stalls.

### Insight 5: For VNE, the "greedy evaluation on a fixed validation set" step is particularly tricky

The greedy evaluation uses the same policy network but with **deterministic selection** (argmax of logits). For VNE:
- Greedy decoding of a moderate-sized VNE instance may produce **infeasible solutions** (runs out of resources due to suboptimal early choices).
- The paper does not specify how infeasible solutions are handled in validation.
- The current VNE implementation scores infeasible solutions with `-inf` or a large negative penalty, which means greedy validation could produce misleadingly poor results.
- This might cause the leader to never update even if the policy improved in expectation.

### Insight 6: The paper assumes problem instances are "random" but the distribution matters enormously

The SIL cycle says "random instances are generated" but does not specify the distribution. For VNE:
- Instance distribution (substrate size, request count, link density, demand ranges) determines what the policy learns.
- The current config defines ranges: 20-40 substrate nodes, 2-6 requests, 2-5 virtual nodes per request. These are the VNE "distribution."
- **Danger:** if the validation set has a different distribution (e.g., denser graphs, harder constraints), the leader may never recognize improvement even when it occurred on the training distribution.

### Insight 7: The cross-entropy loss on "uniformly sampled partial solutions" enables efficient use of each solved instance

Each solved VNE instance with `L` total decisions generates `L` training pairs. For a 50k dataset with an average of ~10 decisions per instance, that's 500k training pairs. Training is **not sample-efficient per instance** (it uses every decision as a training point), but it is **data-efficient** because each instance contributes multiple gradient updates.

## (f) Open Questions This Section Raises

### For the paper (general):

1. **How many solutions K per instance are decoded in Phase 1?** The preliminaries do not specify this. TaSaR has its own beam_width parameter, but the section doesn't say it's the same K. Is K = beam_width, or are multiple TaSaR runs used per instance?

2. **How are infeasible solutions handled during training?** The loss uses `log pi_theta(a_{d+1} | ...)`. If a decoded solution is infeasible, does it get excluded from the training set? Is the objective `f` already `-inf` for infeasible, so they're never the "best solution"?

3. **Is the validation greedy decoding performed once per epoch or more frequently?** The cycle says "after training" but doesn't specify whether intermediate checkpoints are evaluated.

4. **What is the batch size B in practice?** How many (partial_solution, next_action) pairs constitute a batch? This affects the variance of the gradient estimate.

5. **Does the loss weight all positions equally, or is there a discount (like RL)?** The uniform sampling suggests equal weight, which differs from RL where later actions are discounted. This is an implicit design choice that might matter for VNE.

6. **What happens when the leader never updates?** In the worst case, if greedy validation never beats the leader, training continues with stale data forever. Is there a decay mechanism or early stopping?

7. **How does the method scale with n (problem length)?** The autoregressive factorization has computation O(n) per solution. For VNE with long chains (many requests x many links), this is an important practical concern.

### For VNE specifically:

8. **What should the action order be?** The chain formulation fixes order, but for general VNE, is there a principled way to order actions? Does the ordering affect the quality of self-improvement?

9. **How does the uniformly-sampled-partial-solution training interact with VNE's resource constraints?** Late-stage decisions are resource-constrained and may have very few feasible actions. The model must learn to avoid dead ends — does the cross-entropy loss on solved trajectories provide enough signal for this?

10. **Can the leader mechanism compensate for VNE training instability?** The gradient clipping fix addressed the immediate divergence, but the leader mechanism in Gumbeldore mode would add another layer of robustness. Should we switch to Gumbeldore mode sooner rather than later?

11. **What is the right "thousands of instances" target for VNE?** The paper says thousands per epoch are desirable. For the current 50k static dataset, is 50k enough, or is online generation essential? The A/B result suggests 1k and 50k plateau at the same point, so maybe the policy capacity is the bottleneck, not the dataset size.

12. **How does the length assumption affect the VNE network architecture?** The VNE network is a set-based LEHD that doesn't exploit the sequence structure explicitly (no positional encoding). Does the policy lose the ability to condition on earlier actions in a positional way?

## Key Takeaway for VNE Adaptation

The preliminaries present a framework that is **problem-agnostic** and **directly applicable** to VNE, but three VNE-specific challenges are not addressed:

1. **Action space size and structure** (paths, not node selections) — the policy must handle large, variable candidate sets.
2. **Solution equivalence under different decision orders** — the fixed tuple assumption may miss symmetries.
3. **Resource-constrained feasibility** — infeasible partial solutions are more common than in TSP/CVRP, and the framework assumes the policy can distinguish them.

These challenges do not break the framework, but they make it harder for VNE to achieve the same efficiency gains as the paper's benchmark problems.
